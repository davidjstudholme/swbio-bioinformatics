Thursday afternoon (13:30–16:30): Analysis and interpretation
Purpose: produce the first meaningful result and understand what it tells us.
This should be the main practical session. Students work through their proposed workflows, with support available through Teams.
I'd divide the session into three stages.
Stage 1 — Run the analysis (13:30–14:45)
Students continue their chosen mini-project, following the workflow they developed in the morning.
The tasks will differ by project.
Project type	Examples of tasks
RNA-seq	Check reads, map to a reference, quantify expression, investigate differential expression
Genome assembly	Assemble reads, inspect contigs, assess assembly quality
Open-ended project	Implement the first steps of an agreed, feasible workflow
Your coursework already provides downsampled RNA-seq datasets of two million reads per sample to make the exercises quicker. That should help students get results within the available time. SWBio Bioinformatics coursework task 2025.docx

I'd encourage them to use the supplied datasets and existing Galaxy workflows wherever possible, rather than spending the session downloading huge datasets or installing software.
Stage 2 — Turn outputs into figures (15:00–15:45)
Students identify at least one result worth communicating.
Examples include:
- An RNA-seq PCA plot or volcano plot.
- A table of candidate differentially expressed genes.
- A genome assembly summary table.
- A plot or table showing contig lengths or assembly completeness.
The key is not the number of figures. It is whether students can explain what a figure shows and why it is relevant to their question.
Stage 3 — Interpret the result (15:45–16:30)
Ask students to answer three questions in writing:
1. What does my result show?
2. What does it not show?
3. What should I do next?
For example, a student who identifies differentially expressed genes should consider whether the result is statistically supported, whether the sample metadata justify the comparison, and whether the genes have plausible biological interpretations.
A student who assembles a genome should distinguish contiguity from completeness and avoid assuming that a large N50 necessarily means a good assembly.
The deliverable: one preliminary figure and its interpretation
By the end of Thursday, each student should have:
- At least one preliminary result.
- A figure or table.
- A short explanatory caption.
- A few sentences explaining the result and its limitations.
This is a realistic milestone without requiring everyone to finish their analysis.
