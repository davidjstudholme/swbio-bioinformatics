Friday afternoon (13:30–16:30): Communicating the result
Purpose: turn the analysis into a concise, defensible scientific argument.
Your assessment already asks students to produce an A3 poster containing an introduction, methods, results, conclusion and references, with figures and explanatory legends. It recommends a concise poster of around 500 words or fewer, plus figures and tables. SWBio Bioinformatics coursework task 2025.docx

I'd use Friday afternoon as a structured start on that poster, rather than teaching a separate session on presentation design.
Part 1 — Construct the scientific story (13:30–14:00)
Ask each student to complete these sentences:
- Question: I wanted to find out whether…
- Approach: I analysed…
- Result: I found…
- Interpretation: This suggests…
- Limitation: However, I cannot conclude…
If they cannot complete these sentences, they may not yet understand what their analysis has achieved.
Part 2 — Build the poster skeleton (14:00–14:45)
Students create a basic poster structure, even if some results remain preliminary.
I'd ask them to prioritise the scientific content before worrying about colour schemes or decorative graphics.
Section	What it needs to accomplish
Title	State the topic or question clearly
Introduction	Explain the biological context and question
Methods	Describe the data and principal analytical steps
Results	Present the main figure or table with a useful legend
Conclusion	State what the evidence supports, including limitations
References	Credit the original study, datasets and relevant methods
This follows the structure of your existing coursework instructions. SWBio Bioinformatics coursework task 2025.docx

Part 3 — Figure and caption workshop (15:00–15:45)
I'd spend dedicated time on this because figures are often where students struggle most.
Have them check:
- Are axes labelled and units or scales explained?
- Does the legend say what was analysed?
- Are sample sizes or relevant statistical measures reported?
- Can a reader understand the figure without being talked through it?
- Does the caption distinguish observation from interpretation?
For instance, "Volcano plot of differentially expressed genes" is a weak caption. A stronger one would explain the comparison, what the axes represent, how significance was assessed and what the highlighted points indicate.
Part 4 — Short presentation and feedback (15:45–16:30)
Ask each student to present their poster skeleton or preliminary figure in two minutes, followed by one minute of questions.
Peers should focus on three things:
1. Is the biological question clear?
2. Is the main result understandable?
3. Is the conclusion justified by the evidence?
The student then identifies one improvement to make after the course.
The deliverable: a poster skeleton, at least one figure with a draft legend, and a clear statement of the main finding and its limitations.

5. How to make this work with eight different mini-projects
The main logistical challenge is that you will have students doing different things simultaneously. I would address this with a common structure and project-specific support, rather than trying to standardise every student's workflow.
I'd prepare a short project sheet for each mini-project with four headings:
- What biological question could this project answer?
- What is the minimum viable analysis?
- What would a useful result look like?
- What are the main limitations or pitfalls?
For example:
Mini-project	Minimum viable outcome
Bumblebee RNA-seq	A defensible comparison of exposed and control samples, with an interpretable expression result
Arabidopsis RNA-seq	A clearly defined comparison between two relevant conditions
Gyrodactylus transcriptome	A documented exploratory analysis, with the missing treatment metadata explicitly recognised
Burkholderia mutants	A comparison of wild-type and mutant expression, supported by QC and appropriate interpretation
Yeast genome	An assembly and a basic quality assessment
Bacterial genome	An assembly with a clear explanation of its quality and limitations
Chloroplast genome	An assembly attempt with an assessment of whether the resulting sequence is consistent with the intended target
Open-ended project	An agreed, feasible question and a preliminary result from a relevant method
These are suggested teaching milestones, not extra assessment requirements. They build on the projects you already provide. SWBio Bioinformatics coursework task 2025.docx

One important point: not all the RNA-seq projects are equally straightforward statistically. The bumblebee project asks students to compare imidacloprid-exposed and control samples; the Arabidopsis project includes multiple temperature/time conditions; and the Burkholderia project has three wild-type and three mutant samples.  I'd provide a recommended comparison for each, rather than leave students to choose contrasts without guidance.
I'd also make clear that a result does not have to be statistically significant to be a useful outcome. A well-documented analysis that reveals insufficient evidence, missing metadata or a limitation in the data can still demonstrate good bioinformatics practice.
6. What I would prepare before the course
You don't need to create a new set of full tutorials. I'd prioritise five relatively lightweight resources:
1. A common analysis-plan template for Thursday morning.
2. A one-page project guide for each of the eight mini-projects.
3. A critical-analysis checklist for Friday morning.
4. A poster checklist and figure-caption guide for Friday afternoon.
5. A shared Teams support channel where students can post the project they are doing, the step they are attempting, the error or uncertainty they have encountered, and what they have tried.
For yourself, I'd also prepare a short instructor checklist for each project: the likely sticking points, the minimum acceptable outcome and the most important limitation to watch for.
I would not make students submit four separate assessed pieces of work. These should be scaffolding for the existing poster assessment, not additional assignments.
